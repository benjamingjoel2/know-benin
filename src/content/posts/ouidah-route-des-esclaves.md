The road to the sea runs straight and quiet. Behind you is the town, with its temples, churches and old merchant houses; ahead, after a few kilometres of scrub, palms and red earth, is the beach. Somewhere along the way the sound of traffic falls away and you can hear the surf. At the end, an arch stands on the sand, its panels crowded with figures in chains, walking towards the water.

This is the Route des Esclaves in Ouidah, the road that people captured in the interior were forced along to the ships. Walking it is not a sightseeing outing in the usual sense. It is a slow, sometimes heavy few hours, and it is the reason many people come to Benin at all. Here is the history behind it, what you will find at each stop, what else to see in Ouidah, and how to make the visit with the care it asks for.

## Why Ouidah

Ouidah sits on Benin's south-west coast, about thirty kilometres from Cotonou. The government calls it the land of the Xwéda (Hueda) people, whose coast became one of the main departure points of the Atlantic slave trade.

The turning point came in 1727. That year, the [Government of Benin](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1055/) writes, King Agadja of Abomey conquered the Xwéda kingdom of Savi and made Ouidah the maritime gateway of Dahomey. It became the main port on the coast of the Bight of Benin, and the place from which, in the government's words, thousands of enslaved people passed through what is now remembered as the Door of No Return to the Americas.

European powers built forts here to trade. The government lists Portuguese, French, English, Dutch and Danish forts in the town. Their presence, and the trade they served, is why Ouidah's memorials matter so much to people across the Atlantic world today.

## The UNESCO connection

Ouidah also has a place in how the world remembers the slave trade. UNESCO's Slave Route project, now called Routes of Enslaved Peoples, was launched in Ouidah in 1994 ([gouv.bj](https://www.gouv.bj/actualite/323/lunesco-aux-cotes-du-benin-pour-la-celebration-du-25eme-anniversaire-du-projet-la-route-de-lesclave); [The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/benin-is-building-a-theme-park-to-remember-slavery-is-history-up-for-sale-196405)). The Door of No Return arch was erected on the beach the following year, in 1995 ([Afrik.com](https://www.afrik.com/benin-a-ouidah-la-route-de-l-esclave)).

The government notes that 23 August, the International Day for the Remembrance of the Slave Trade and its Abolition, is traditionally marked in Ouidah ([gouv.bj](https://www.gouv.bj/actualite/323/lunesco-aux-cotes-du-benin-pour-la-celebration-du-25eme-anniversaire-du-projet-la-route-de-lesclave)). If you are in Benin around that date, it is a meaningful time to visit.

## Walking the route, stop by stop

The government describes the Route de l'Esclave as a walk of about three kilometres from the Place aux Enchères to the Door of No Return ([gouv.bj](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1055/)). The road has been marked with sculptures and symbolic stops commemorating the march of the captives ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/benin-is-building-a-theme-park-to-remember-slavery-is-history-up-for-sale-196405)). The stops below follow the order given by the government and by [Afrik.com](https://www.afrik.com/benin-a-ouidah-la-route-de-l-esclave).

### Place aux Enchères

The walk begins at the auction square, also known as Place Chacha. This is where captives were traded, and, according to Afrik.com, branded with hot irons.

### The Tree of Forgetting

The next stop is the Arbre de l'Oubli. The tradition recounted here is that captives were made to walk around a tree, with different numbers of turns for men and women, so that they would forget their past.

### Case Zomaï

The Zomaï enclosure was a place of confinement. The government describes a dark room where captives were held for days so that they would lose all memory of their former lives.

### The Zoungbodji memorial

At Zoungbodji, a memorial marks the site of a mass grave, where those who died or fell too sick to continue were thrown. Nearby, a monument called Zomaci was raised by the Movement for Repentance, Forgiveness and Reconciliation ([Afrik.com](https://www.afrik.com/benin-a-ouidah-la-route-de-l-esclave)). The government also lists a Mémorial du Repentir, a memorial of repentance, on the route.

### The Tree of Return

Before the shore comes the Tree of Return. Here the tradition is the opposite of the Tree of Forgetting: a ritual meant to ensure that the soul could find rest and return home after death.

### The Door of No Return

The route ends on the beach at the Porte du Non-Retour. The arch is about 17 metres tall and has recently been restored; its carved panels show figures in chains walking towards the ocean ([Africanews](https://www.africanews.com/2026/04/13/benin-leans-into-painful-past-to-encourage-cultural-tourism/)).

A word on what you are seeing. Some of these stops mark documented places; others hold stories that have been passed down and shaped into a memorial over generations. Guides tell them in different ways, and details such as the number of turns around the trees vary between accounts. That doesn't make them less meaningful, but it helps to hear them as memory as well as record. A good local historian will tell you which is which.

## Ouidah's other sites

### The Ouidah History Museum

The town's history museum is housed in the former Portuguese fort ([gouv.bj](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1055/)). The fort was being rehabilitated in 2022, with work on the governor's house and chapel and new visitor spaces ([gouv.bj](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1644/reconstruction-identique-cite-historique-ouidah-nouvelle-descente-ministres-chantiers-construction-infrastructures-touristiques-museales/)). Check what is open before you go. The government also lists the de Souza family museum and a Musée de la Porte du Retour in the town.

### The Python Temple and the basilica

In the centre of Ouidah, the Temple of Pythons honours Dangbé, the python divinity. The government says three families still worship Dangbé there. Directly across the square stands the Catholic basilica, built about a century ago, facing the temple in what the government calls a sign of peaceful coexistence between religions ([gouv.bj](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1055/)). Standing between the two is one of the clearest ways to understand how faiths sit side by side in Benin.

![The square in Ouidah between the Python Temple and the Catholic basilica](photo:python-temple)

Vodun is a living religion, and the temple is a place of worship, not a museum. Follow the instructions of the people who look after it, and ask before taking photographs inside.

### Afro-Brazilian Ouidah

Walk the older streets and you will see houses built in a Brazilian style, and the government notes that many local families carry Portuguese or Afro-Brazilian surnames, such as da Silva and d'Oliveira ([gouv.bj](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1055/)). These names and buildings are part of a two-way story across the Atlantic: some Ouidah families descend from formerly enslaved people who returned from Brazil ([NPR](https://www.weku.org/npr-news/2026-08-12/the-commercialization-of-the-west-african-coasts-slavery-heritage)). Ask a guide to point out the houses, and to explain which families are linked to them.

## A memorial landscape under construction

Ouidah is changing fast, and you should know what to expect. The government is building a large memorial and tourism complex called La Marina at Djègbadji, near the Door of No Return. Plans described in reporting include hotels, a Vodun arena, memorial gardens and a life-size replica of a slave ship ([The Conversation](https://theconversation.com/benin-is-building-a-theme-park-to-remember-slavery-is-history-up-for-sale-196405)). The replica is of L'Aurore, one of the last three-masted ships to leave Ouidah for Cuba around 1860, and it is due to open as an immersive museum of the slave trade, without a confirmed date at the time of writing ([Africanews](https://www.africanews.com/2026/04/13/benin-leans-into-painful-past-to-encourage-cultural-tourism/)).

In August 2026, [NPR reported](https://www.weku.org/npr-news/2026-08-12/the-commercialization-of-the-west-african-coasts-slavery-heritage) that construction was still under way, that ground around the Tree of Forgetting was being paved, and that plaques and monuments along the route were being restored or replaced. A local guide quoted in the report said a new gate is planned to replace the current arch.

The project has its critics. Some worry about mass tourism's effect on the coastal environment, and others question whether replicas and resorts are the right way to remember the trade. Expect building work, closed sections and changes to the route, and ask locally what is open.

## Staying safe on the beach

The Door of No Return stands on an open Atlantic beach, and the sea here is dangerous. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office says ocean currents are very strong along all parts of Benin's coast and that many drownings occur each year. It advises not swimming unless you have reliable local advice and confidence in your ability, and not walking on the beach alone at any time ([FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/safety-and-security)).

Shade is limited, so bring water and a hat. Check the [FCDO's Benin advice](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin), or your own government's, before you travel, and read our guide to [Benin's e-visa and entry requirements](/stories/benin-e-visa-entry-requirements/).

## Visiting with respect

- **Go with a guide.** A knowledgeable local guide or historian brings the stops to life and can explain the difference between documented history and remembered tradition.
- **Give it time.** We'd allow a full morning for the route itself, and avoid squeezing it in between other sights.
- **Mind your camera.** Photographs of the monuments are generally fine, but ask before photographing people, especially anyone visibly grieving or praying. Inside temples, ask first.
- **Keep your voice down** at the memorials, and let other visitors have their moment.
- **Think about the dates.** In early January, Ouidah fills for the [Vodun Days festival](/stories/vodun-days-ouidah/). If you want quiet at the Door of No Return, come at another time; if you want to see the town at its most alive, come then and walk the route on a separate day.

## Where this fits in a trip

Ouidah is an easy day from Cotonou, but it is better with a night or two, so that you can walk the route early, before the heat and the day visitors. Pair it with the [royal palaces of Abomey](/stories/royal-palaces-of-abomey/), the seat of the kings who made Ouidah their port, for a fuller picture of Dahomey. Many travellers also continue along the coast into Togo and Ghana, where the forts of Cape Coast tell another chapter of the same story; see [combining Benin with Togo and Ghana](/stories/benin-togo-ghana/).

For travellers of African descent in particular, this can be a personal journey as much as a historical one. A local operator who works with historians and community members in Ouidah can shape the visit around what you want to understand, and give you the time to take it in.
