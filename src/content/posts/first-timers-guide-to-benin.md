The sail is mostly patches, stitched from whatever cloth was to hand, and it fills with a soft push of wind off the lagoon. Ahead, the stilt houses of Ganvié rise out of the water on their thin legs. Behind you, Cotonou hums: motorbikes, market traffic, the long low skyline of a port city. In an hour you have crossed from one Benin into another, and that, more than anything, is what a first trip here feels like.

Benin is a small country with a great deal packed into it: the old kingdom of Danxomè, the memorial road of the slave trade at Ouidah, a living Vodun religion, and lagoon villages that have lived on the water for generations. This guide covers what we think a first-time visitor needs to know, and where to begin.

## Where Benin is, and how it is laid out

Benin sits on the Gulf of Guinea in West Africa, a narrow country stretching inland from a short Atlantic coast. France Diplomatie's [country profile](https://www.diplomatie.gouv.fr/fr/information-par-pays/benin/presentation-du-benin) gives its area as 114,763 km², names Porto-Novo as the official capital and lists Cotonou, Porto-Novo and Parakou as the main cities.

For a first-time visitor, the useful division is between south and north. The south is where most first trips happen: the coast and lagoons around Cotonou, Porto-Novo and Ganvié, Ouidah to the west, and the royal city of Abomey inland. The north, with the Atakora hills and the national parks, is a different landscape and, at the time of writing, a different risk picture (see the safety section below).

## When to go

Benin has two rainy seasons in the south and one in the north. According to [France Diplomatie](https://www.diplomatie.gouv.fr/fr/information-par-pays/benin/conseils-aux-voyageurs-risques-naturels), the southern rains run from April to mid-July and again from mid-September to October, while the north's rainy season runs from June to September. Heavy rain can cause flooding, especially in rural areas, and roads can become impassable. From December to February the harmattan, a dust-laden wind, can reduce visibility.

That leaves the months from November to March outside the rainy seasons, which is when we'd aim for a first visit. Early January has its own pull: the government's [Ouidah page](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1055/) describes 10 January as a national Vodun day, the focus of the festival we cover in [The Vodun Days festival in Ouidah](/stories/vodun-days-ouidah/). The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/safety-and-security) warns that accommodation is limited around the festival and pickpockets are active, so book early.

## Getting in

Most visitors need a visa, and the usual route is Benin's official e-visa. In short: apply online at [evisa.bj](https://evisa.bj/), choose between 30-day and 90-day options, and allow time. The portal's [FAQ](https://evisa.bj/foire-aux-questions) recommends applying at least 7 days ahead, and the [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/entry-requirements) says at least 4 working days. The same FAQ says Africans can visit for 90 days without a visa. Fees, passport rules and the step-by-step process are all in our full guide, [Benin's e-visa and entry, explained](/stories/benin-e-visa-entry-requirements/).

## Money

Benin's currency is the CFA franc, according to [France Diplomatie](https://www.diplomatie.gouv.fr/fr/information-par-pays/benin/presentation-du-benin). Benin is a member of the West African Economic and Monetary Union (UEMOA).

France Diplomatie's [security advice](https://www.diplomatie.gouv.fr/fr/information-par-pays/benin/conseils-aux-voyageurs-securite) recommends using indoor cash machines where possible, not carrying large sums, not handling large amounts of cash in public, and leaving valuables in a hotel safe. We'd also carry some cash for small purchases, especially outside Cotonou.

## Languages

French is the official language. France Diplomatie lists French, Fon and Yoruba as languages in everyday use. Learning to greet people in French goes a long way, and a word or two of Fon, picked up from whoever is showing you around, is always welcome.

## Getting around

This is the section to read carefully, because road travel is where most visitors are most exposed to risk.

- **Motorbike taxis.** Zémidjans, the motorbike taxis that fill Cotonou's streets, are everywhere. [France Diplomatie](https://www.diplomatie.gouv.fr/fr/information-par-pays/benin/conseils-aux-voyageurs-informations-utiles) recommends using car taxis in Cotonou and avoiding motorbike taxis; if you do use one, it says a good-quality helmet is essential, because road accidents are frequent.
- **After dark.** The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/safety-and-security) advises avoiding driving outside towns and cities at night, as roads are poorly lit, and keeping doors locked and windows closed when driving. France Diplomatie also advises against travelling at night.
- **Public transport.** The FCDO says there is no reliable public transport in Benin and advises avoiding taxis and long-distance buses, which it describes as poorly maintained and often overloaded.
- **Driving yourself.** Both the FCDO and France Diplomatie say you need an international driving permit; France Diplomatie adds that local insurance is required.

In practice, most first-time visitors are better off with a car and a local driver arranged through an operator or a reliable hotel, and day-time journeys between towns.

## Health

Start your health planning at least eight weeks before you travel, as the [FCDO's health page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/health) suggests.

**Yellow fever.** The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/entry-requirements) and [France Diplomatie](https://www.diplomatie.gouv.fr/fr/information-par-pays/benin/conseils-aux-voyageurs-sante) both say you need a yellow fever vaccination certificate to enter Benin. In September 2026 Benin's Ministry of Health [announced](https://evisa.bj/articles/controle-du-carnet-de-vaccination-fievre-jaune-aux-points-dentree-du-benin/0225cd59-9365-4534-94ee-c1e3cdc996c7) that vaccination records are no longer checked at entry points, while stressing that vaccination remains recommended and the check could return at any time. [TravelHealthPro](https://travelhealthpro.org.uk/country/27/benin) says there is a risk of yellow fever transmission throughout the country. Get vaccinated and carry the certificate.

**Malaria.** TravelHealthPro describes a high risk of malaria in Benin and recommends antimalarial tablets, and the [US CDC](https://wwwnc.cdc.gov/travel/destinations/traveler/none/benin) recommends prescription medicine to prevent it. France Diplomatie notes that Benin is in a zone of high drug resistance, so the choice of tablets needs medical advice. Bite avoidance matters too: TravelHealthPro also flags a risk of dengue.

**Care and insurance.** The FCDO says medical facilities are poor, particularly in rural areas, and that serious treatment may mean medical evacuation. Make sure your travel insurance covers it. The FCDO gives 118 as the number to call for an ambulance.

## Safety, in short

Read your own government's advice before you book. Here is the outline from the UK's [Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin), at the time of writing (November 2026).

**The north.** The FCDO advises against all travel to Pendjari National Park and its hunting zones, the Parc du W and its neighbouring hunting zones, and anywhere within 5 km of the Burkina Faso border. It advises against all but essential travel to the rest of the Alibori and Atacora departments, and to the area between the RNIE 2 highway and the Nigerian border. It says your travel insurance could be invalidated if you travel against its advice. [France Diplomatie](https://www.diplomatie.gouv.fr/fr/information-par-pays/benin/conseils-aux-voyageurs-securite) also advises strongly against the border areas and both national parks. This is one reason we suggest a first trip stays in the south.

**Cotonou.** The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/safety-and-security) says street crime, including theft and mugging, is common in Cotonou. It advises being alert in Dantokpa market and avoiding the area at night, not walking alone at night, and never walking on the beach alone.

![The Dantokpa market building seen across the lagoon, Cotonou](photo:dantokpa)

**The sea.** The FCDO says ocean currents are very strong along the whole coast and that many people drown each year. France Diplomatie advises against swimming off the coast at any time of year. Admire the surf; don't swim in it.

**Unrest.** The FCDO notes an attempted coup on 7 December, which [France Diplomatie](https://www.diplomatie.gouv.fr/fr/information-par-pays/benin/presentation-du-benin) dates to 2025, and advises avoiding demonstrations and large political gatherings, and following any curfews.

## Etiquette and photography

Greetings come first in Benin. Before you ask for directions, a price or a photograph, say hello and take a moment over it. It is the simplest way to show respect, and things tend to go better for it.

Vodun is a living religion, practised by many Beninese, not a performance put on for visitors. The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/safety-and-security) asks travellers to research and respect religious and social traditions. [France Diplomatie](https://www.diplomatie.gouv.fr/fr/information-par-pays/benin/conseils-aux-voyageurs-informations-utiles) advises discretion about religion and says you should not photograph traditional ceremonies or markets without permission. In practice, that means asking before you photograph people, shrines and ceremonies, and accepting a no gracefully. Some places will not allow cameras at all.

There are legal limits too. The FCDO says it is illegal to photograph sensitive sites such as military installations or the airport, and France Diplomatie says filming public infrastructure or flying a drone needs prior permission.

## Where to start

Most first trips begin in Cotonou and make a loop through the south. Five places to build around:

- **Cotonou.** One of Benin's main cities and the usual arrival point, with the vast Dantokpa market on the lagoon. Give it a day to find your feet.
- **Porto-Novo.** The official capital, close to Cotonou and a quieter counterpoint to it, easily paired with a day on the lagoons.
- **Ganvié.** The village on stilts on Lake Nokoué, reached by pirogue. Read [Ganvié, the village on stilts](/stories/ganvie-village-on-stilts/) before you go.
- **Ouidah.** About 30 km from Cotonou, according to the [government](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1055/), and home to the 3 km Route des Esclaves that ends at the Door of No Return. Our guide is [Ouidah and the Route des Esclaves](/stories/ouidah-route-des-esclaves/).
- **Abomey.** Inland, the seat of the kings of Danxomè, whose palace site is [inscribed by UNESCO](https://www.gouv.bj/actualite/2188/promotion-tourisme-autour-site-palatial-abomey-bientot-grand-musee-cinq-palais-royaux-rehabilites/) and is the focus of a large government restoration project, so check what is open before you go. See [The royal palaces of Abomey](/stories/royal-palaces-of-abomey/).

## Planning with a local operator

For a first trip, a Cotonou-based operator can handle the parts that are hardest to arrange from abroad: a driver for day-time road journeys, guides who know when and where photographs are welcome, and up-to-date knowledge of which areas are affected by current travel advice. Ask any operator how they follow official advice before you book.
