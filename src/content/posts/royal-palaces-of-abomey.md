The afternoon heat settles on a courtyard of packed red earth. Low walls run off in every direction, the same colour as the ground they were raised from. Under a tall roof, a row of painted clay panels catches the light: a figure with a weapon, an animal, a sign you cannot yet read. Each one was made to be read. Together they are a history of a kingdom, written on its walls.

This is Abomey, about 145 km north of Cotonou according to the [Government of Benin](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1018/destination-benin---abomey-intraitable-cite-conquerants-%22l-empire-romain%22-golfe-guinee/), and for close to three centuries the seat of the kings of Danxomè, the kingdom Europeans called Dahomey. It is one of the most important historic sites in West Africa, and right now it is in the middle of its biggest change in a generation.

## The kingdom of Danxomè

UNESCO's summary is short and striking: from 1625 to 1900, twelve kings succeeded one another at the head of the kingdom of Abomey. The Government of Benin's own account traces the founding to King Houégbadja, who gave the kingdom a code of laws and set his capital on the Abomey plateau. It names Tassi Hangbé as the only woman to sit on the throne, and Guézo, the ninth sovereign, as the king who turned the economy towards palm oil.

That same account is frank about the rest. Danxomè was built on almost 300 years of conquest, and it prospered from the slave trade. The [Government of Benin](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1055/) notes that King Agadja of Abomey made Ouidah his seaport in 1727, and that thousands of enslaved people, captured inland or taken as prisoners of war, were shipped from there to the Americas. If you are also visiting Ouidah, read our guide to [Ouidah and the Route des Esclaves](/stories/ouidah-route-des-esclaves/) alongside this one; the two places tell two sides of one history.

The kingdom ended with the French colonial conquest in the 1890s. The government's account puts it simply: the invasion ended the reign of King Béhanzin (Gbéhanzin).

## A UNESCO World Heritage Site, and a fragile one

The Royal Palaces of Abomey were inscribed on the [UNESCO World Heritage List](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/323/) in 1985. UNESCO describes the site as the major material testimony to the kingdom: a set of palaces, built one after another by successive kings, mostly within a single earthen-walled enclosure, with King Akaba's palace set apart in its own.

They went straight onto the List of World Heritage in Danger in the same year. A tornado in 1984 had badly damaged the palaces, and UNESCO also cited restoration work that had not respected the original materials, volumes and colours. It took more than two decades of conservation work, partly funded by the World Heritage Fund, before the World Heritage Committee [removed the site from the Danger List in June 2007](https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/356).

The respite did not last long. In January 2009 [a fire destroyed several buildings](https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/488), and UNESCO's later monitoring reports continued to raise concerns about the state of conservation. Earth architecture needs constant care. Walls built from the ground return to it if the roofs fail or the rains are hard. Keep that in mind as you walk: what you see is not a ruin frozen in time, but something people are actively keeping alive.

## Reading the walls: the bas-reliefs

The painted clay panels are what most visitors remember. They are bas-reliefs, low sculptures in earth set into the palace walls and painted in colour.

In the 1990s the Getty Conservation Institute worked with Benin's cultural heritage department to conserve fifty badly damaged panels from the palaces. The [project records](https://oac.cdlib.org/findaid/ark:/13030/c8mg7r14/) describe them as polychrome earthen panels, each about three feet (roughly a metre) square, that depict battle scenes and allegorical symbols of the power of the kings of Dahomey. The same source notes that the bas-reliefs are thought to be the oldest surviving parts of the palaces.

![A building at the Abomey palaces with a tall pyramidal corrugated roof above a row of painted bas-relief panels](photo:abomey-hut)

Because so much of their meaning is symbolic, a good local guide is the difference between looking at the panels and understanding them. Ask whose reign a panel belongs to, what the symbol stands for, and how the story is still told in Abomey today.

## The Agojie, carefully

You will hear about the women soldiers of Dahomey in Abomey, and you may have seen the 2022 film *The Woman King*. The history is real, but it is more complicated than the film.

[Smithsonian Magazine](https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/the-true-history-of-the-woman-king-dahomey-180980750/) summarises what historians know. The unit is usually called the Agojie; Europeans called them "Amazons". Their first recorded mention dates to 1729, and their origins are uncertain: they may have begun as a corps of women elephant hunters, or as a palace guard. King Ghezo formally made them part of the army, and they were at their height in the nineteenth century. The women were considered *ahosi*, wives of the king.

The same article is clear that the Agojie took part in Dahomey's slave raids, and quotes a historian who doubts the film's picture of them as opponents of the slave trade. The war with France in 1892 all but destroyed them: Smithsonian reports that of roughly 1,200 Agojie fit to fight at its start, only 50 to 60 were left by the end. The Government of Benin's own account of Abomey calls them legendary battalions of women warriors, and the name of the new museum gives them top billing. Hold both truths at once: these were remarkable soldiers, and they were part of a state that enslaved people.

## The 2021 restitution and the new museum

When French forces conquered Abomey in 1892, objects from the royal treasures were taken as spoils of war; the Élysée describes them as war booty of Colonel (later General) Dodds. In November 2021 France returned 26 of them. The [Élysée](https://www.elysee.fr/en/emmanuel-macron/2021/11/09/declaration-du-president-emmanuel-macron-et-du-president-du-benin-patrice-talon) records that the act transferring ownership was signed in Paris on 9 November 2021, and [Al Jazeera](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2021/11/9/france-hands-back-26-treasures-looted-from-benin) reported that the pieces included a royal throne and three totemic statues, to be shown at sites including Ouidah's old Portuguese fort while a museum in Abomey was completed.

That museum is the centre of a large redevelopment. The [Government of Benin](https://www.gouv.bj/article/2188/promotion-tourisme-autour-site-palatial-abomey-bientot-grand-musee-cinq-palais-royaux-rehabilites/) set out the plan in April 2023: a new museum to international standards, the Musée des Rois et des Amazones du Danxomè; the rehabilitation of five royal palaces (those of Ghézo, Glèlè, Béhanzin, Agoli-Agbo and Akaba, with Queen Hangbé); and a heritage walk around the site. It put the total cost at 30 billion CFA francs, 23 billion of it from the Agence Française de Développement. The [AFD's project page](https://www.afd.fr/fr/carte-des-projets/musee-dabomey-promouvoir-le-patrimoine-historique-et-culturel-au-benin-muard) describes a 4,000 m² museum designed to resemble a royal palace, where the 26 returned works will be displayed, plus restoration of the palaces and their enclosure wall.

### What is open right now?

Honestly, we could not confirm it. In April 2023 the government said the work would take about two and a half years. At the time of writing (November 2026) we found no official announcement that the new museum has opened to the public, and our sister site [Trusted Ground](https://trustedground.tourground.com/articles/benin-abomey-ouidah/) makes the same point: parts of the site may be closed, moved or presented differently while work goes on.

So ask before you go:

- Which palaces and courtyards are open to visitors this week?
- Is the new museum open, and are the returned treasures on display there or elsewhere?
- Are the bas-reliefs you see originals or replicas, and where are the originals kept?
- Do you need to book a guide in advance, and is photography allowed inside?

A local operator or your hotel in Abomey will know the current picture better than any guidebook.

## Getting there from Cotonou

Abomey is a long day trip from Cotonou, but it rewards a night or two. We would stay over: the light on the red walls is best early and late, and the town has more to it than the palace site.

Most visitors travel by private car with a driver, or on a tour. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office (FCDO) advises [avoiding driving outside towns and cities at night](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/safety-and-security), as roads are poorly lit, so plan to leave Cotonou in the morning and arrive in daylight. The same page notes that driving standards and road conditions are poor. Abomey is in the centre-south of the country, outside the northern areas where the FCDO currently advises against travel, but always check the [latest FCDO advice for Benin](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin) or your own government's advice before you set off.

Abomey fits naturally into a wider route. Our [10-day Benin itinerary](/stories/benin-10-day-itinerary/) gives it two days, between the coast and the hills of Dassa-Zoumé.

## Visiting respectfully

Abomey is a living town, not only a heritage site, and the Government of Benin notes that Vodun traditions remain strong here, including the cults of Lègba and Gou. Treat shrines and sacred spaces with the same care you would a church or mosque.

- Follow your guide's lead on where you may walk, and take off your shoes if asked.
- Ask before photographing people, shrines and ceremonies, and accept a no.
- Dress modestly; shoulders and knees covered is a safe default.
- If you are invited into a family compound or an artisan's workshop, treat it as you would a visit to someone's home.
- Remember whose history this is. The kingdom's wars and its role in the slave trade are part of the story Abomey tells, and the descendants of both the powerful and the captured live in Benin today.

## Before you go

The palaces of Abomey are at a turning point: restored treasures coming home, new buildings rising, old ones being repaired. That makes it an exciting moment to visit, and an uncertain one to plan. We would build in a spare half-day in case a part of the site is closed, and talk to a local operator about what is open before you fix your dates.
