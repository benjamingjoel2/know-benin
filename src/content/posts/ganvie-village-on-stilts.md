The pirogue pushes off from the jetty at Abomey-Calavi and the noise of the road falls away behind you. The water is flat and brown, and wide enough that the far shore is only a line. Then the first roofs appear: thatch and corrugated iron, raised on stilts above the lake, with narrow dugouts tied up beneath them. A woman paddles past with her boat low in the water under a load of goods. Someone is mending a net on a veranda. A whole town is going about its morning, and none of it touches dry land.

This is Ganvié, on Lake Nokoué, just north of Cotonou. It is often called the "Venice of Africa", which is a fair description of the canals of water between the houses and a poor one of everything else. Ganvié was not built for beauty or trade. It was built to survive.

## "We survived": how Ganvié began

The founding story is one of escape. Accounts agree on the outline. The Tofinu, a people of the Benin coast known for their skill as fishermen, moved out onto the lake to escape raids by soldiers of the Fon and Dahomey kingdoms, who captured people and sold them to Portuguese slave traders. [ArchDaily](https://www.archdaily.com/1000658/the-floating-village-of-ganvie-a-model-for-socio-ecological-urbanism) dates the settlement to the seventeenth century, [Euronews](https://www.euronews.com/2022/04/30/venice-of-africa-this-village-in-the-middle-of-a-lake-has-a-unique-story-to-tell) to around 400 years ago, and the French development agency [AFD](https://www.afd.fr/en/actualites/reinventing-venice-africa) to the eighteenth century. The exact date is uncertain; the reason is not.

Why the water? The sources say the raiders would not follow. Euronews and the AFD both relate that the soldiers believed a demon lived beneath the surface of the lake; ArchDaily puts it as a religious prohibition that kept the Fon from attacking people on the water. Either way, a shallow lake became a fortress. ArchDaily notes that Lake Nokoué is only one to two metres deep, shallow enough to drive stilts into the bed.

The name carries the memory. Both the AFD and Euronews say Ganvié means "we survived" in the local language.

It is worth holding that in mind as you visit. Ganvié is a monument to people who refused to be captured, in a country where the slave trade shaped so much else. ## How a visit works

Visits start from the jetty (*embarcadère*) at Abomey-Calavi, on the lake shore just north of Cotonou. The Government of Benin has been reorganising tourism here: it reported in [2021](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1587/mise-tourisme-rapide-embarcadere-abomey-calavi-descente-ministre-abimbola-station-touristique/) on renovation works and a new ticketing system at the jetty, and in [2022](https://www.gouv.bj/article/2064/reinventer-cite-lacustre-ganvie-efforts-gouvernement-portent-leurs-fruits/) said the jetty had been rehabilitated and new rules introduced on visiting hours, tariffs and guiding, with the stated aim of professionalising guides and ending scams against visitors. The government describes the crossing to Ganvié as about fifteen minutes on the water.

In practice, that means:

- **Buy your ticket at the official counter** at the jetty rather than agreeing a price with someone who approaches you on the road. Fees change, so we have not listed them here; check at the counter on the day.
- **Ask about the boat.** Depending on what is on offer, you may be able to choose between a motorboat and a paddled pirogue. A paddled boat is slower and quieter, which suits the narrow water between the houses.
- **Go with a guide.** A local guide can explain what you are seeing, help you ask permission to take photos, and take you to places you would not find on your own.
- **Go early.** Light is softer, the heat is gentler and the lake is at its busiest with people going about their day. Escale Voyages' journey, for instance, crosses before sunrise.
- **Bring** a hat, sunscreen, water, small notes for purchases, and a dry bag for your phone or camera.

Plan on half a day for the round trip from Cotonou, including the drive to the jetty.

## Life on the water

Everything here happens on stilts or in boats. Euronews lists the markets, schools, churches, businesses and a hospital built above the lake, and notes that pirogues are the only means of getting around.

### Fishing and the acadjas

Fishing is the old heart of the economy, and the AFD says the population still depends heavily on it. As you cross the lake, look out for patches of branches standing in the water. These are *acadjas*, a form of fish farming. ArchDaily describes how palm fronds and other plant material are anchored into the lakebed and fenced with bamboo; as the material decays it feeds plankton and algae, which draw in fish. The enclosures are rebuilt roughly every two weeks as the fish are harvested.

### The floating market

The market is the place to see Ganvié at its busiest. Euronews describes vendors selling their goods from pirogues at a floating market in the village. If you want to buy something, it is a good way to put money directly into the village. Ask your guide how prices work before you start.

### Pressures on the lake

Ganvié is not a museum. It is a crowded, working town facing real problems. The AFD describes the lake as having no filtration system, with organic waste and wastewater flowing straight into it, and fish stocks threatened by water hyacinth, backfilling, pollution and overfishing. It also notes that tourism now rivals fishing as a source of income. That is a reason to visit well: through official channels, with a local guide, and with money spent in the village.

## The government's redevelopment of Ganvié

Ganvié is the focus of a major government project, "Réinventer la Cité lacustre de Ganvié" (Reinventing the lake city of Ganvié), backed by the AFD. The AFD says it was launched in 2019 to preserve, renovate and develop the village's infrastructure, including night lighting, model houses for rehabilitating fragile buildings, and monitoring of water pollution, alongside work to harvest and recycle water hyacinth.

On 31 October 2025, the [Government of Benin](https://www.gouv.bj/article/3334/programme-action-gouvernement-projet-reinventer-ganvie-transforme-durablement-ava/) launched works on a new road between Akassato and Sô-Ava, with a 16-month timetable, and presented new community buildings at Ganvié, including a youth centre, an artisan centre, a community square and a floating market. The government describes Ganvié as one of seven tourism poles in Benin's offer, and its aim as developing sustainable tourism once residents have a viable living environment.

What this means for your visit is a moving target. Some facilities may be new; some areas may be under works. At the time of writing (November 2026), ask your guide or operator what has opened and what has changed.

## Photography and privacy

Ganvié is many people's home, and for years visitors have arrived with cameras pointed straight into it. Please don't be that visitor.

- **Ask before you photograph anyone**, and accept a no without argument. Your guide can help you ask.
- **Don't photograph through doorways or windows**, or into people's homes, even if they are open to the water.
- **Be especially careful with children.** Ask a parent, or don't take the photo.
- **Wide shots of the lake and the stilt houses** are generally the respectful choice when you have not spoken to anyone.
- **If someone asks for payment** for a portrait, decide before you lift the camera whether you are willing; don't take the shot and then walk away.
- **Put the camera down for a while.** The best part of Ganvié is often the slow ride between the houses, listening.

Vodun is a living religion across southern Benin. If you pass a shrine, ask before photographing it.

## Combining Ganvié with Cotonou and Porto-Novo

Because the jetty is so close to Cotonou, Ganvié fits into almost any trip to southern Benin. A half-day visit in the morning leaves the afternoon free for Cotonou itself.

Porto-Novo, Benin's capital, is about 30 km from Cotonou according to the [Government of Benin](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1048/destination-benin---porto-novo--condense-histoire-culture-fete-adoucit-moeurs/), with its Afro-Brazilian Great Mosque, the Honmè museum and the palace of King Toffa I. Ganvié and Porto-Novo make a natural pair over two days. Our [10-day Benin itinerary](/stories/benin-10-day-itinerary/) puts them together near the start of the trip, and our [first-timer's guide to Benin](/stories/first-timers-guide-to-benin/) covers the practical basics of arriving in Cotonou.

## Planning your visit

Ganvié is easy to reach and easy to do badly. Go early, go with a local guide, buy your ticket through the official jetty, and give the people who live there the same courtesy you would want on your own street. A Cotonou-based operator can arrange the boat, the guide and the timing, and fold it into a few days on the coast.
