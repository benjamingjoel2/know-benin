The drums reach you before the crowd does. You walk down the long, straight road towards the sea at Ouidah, and the sound thickens: a beat from one side, a different beat from the other, whistles, a brass band somewhere behind. Then the road opens onto sand, the Door of No Return rises against a white sky, and the beach in front of it is full of people. Some are in their best cloth, some are in white, some are visitors with cameras still in their bags, waiting to understand what they are looking at.

That moment of not quite understanding is a good place to start. Vodun Days is a festival, with concerts and food stalls and a festival village, but it grows out of something older and more serious. This guide explains what you are walking into, what happens, how to behave, and how to plan the practical side, which in January means booking early.

## What Vodun is

Vodun is a living religion, practised every day across southern Benin and its neighbours, not a historical curiosity or a show put on for visitors. The word means "spirit" or "deity" in Fon, one of Benin's main languages, and the faith is followed by Fon, Aja and Ewe communities in Benin, Togo, Ghana and Nigeria, [as Afar's recent feature explains](https://www.afar.com/magazine/what-is-vodun-how-benin-celebrates-its-spirit-religion). The religions that crossed the Atlantic with enslaved people, among them Haitian Vodou and Brazilian Candomblé, are its descendants.

Ouidah holds a particular place in it. The [Government of Benin](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1055/) calls the town the world-renowned home of Vodun and a place of pilgrimage for Vodun congregations from around the world each year. Its Python Temple honours Dangbé, the python divinity, and still faces the Catholic basilica across a shared square, a pairing the government presents as a sign of peaceful coexistence between faiths.

### The national day, and why the date moved

For decades, Benin marked its national day for traditional religions on 10 January. The government's [Ouidah profile](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1055/) says the day was instituted under President Nicéphore Soglo. That fixed date has now changed. Law no. 2024-32 of 2 September 2024 sets the annual festival of traditional religions ([La Nouvelle Tribune](https://lanouvelletribune.info/2026/01/fete-du-vodun-au-benin-de-la-journee-unique-a-un-format-elargi/)), and under it the public holiday falls on the second Friday of January, with the Thursday before it also a paid holiday ([Bénin Web TV](https://beninwebtv.bj/calendrier-des-vodun-days-2027-et-regime-des-jours-chomes-au-benin/)). In 2027 that means Thursday 7 and Friday 8 January.

You will still see "10 January" in older guidebooks and on many tour pages. Treat it as history, and plan around the current calendar.

## What Vodun Days is

Vodun Days is the government's name for the national celebration in Ouidah. It was announced in late 2023 to replace the annual Vodun festival in the town under a new name, with the first edition on 9 and 10 January 2024. The Minister of Tourism, Culture and Arts said the aim was to reach a wider international audience and to present Benin as the cradle of Vodun and a place of pilgrimage ([gouv.bj](https://www.gouv.bj/article/2481/initiative-vodun-days-ministre-abimbola-devoile-contexte-contenu-allechant-festivites/)).

It has grown since. The 2026 edition, the third, ran from 8 to 10 January ([Bénin Tourisme](https://benin.bj/en/news/vodun-days-2026)).

### Vodun Days 2027 dates

The [official Vodun Days site](https://www.vodundays.bj) gives the dates of the fourth edition as **2 to 9 January 2027**, in Ouidah. That is a big change: the festival stretches from three days to a full week. At a media briefing in August 2026, the government spokesperson said the longer format is meant to keep diaspora visitors in Benin longer and to spread visitors across the week, and that it creates no new public holidays. Monday 4 to Wednesday 6 January stay ordinary working days ([Bénin Web TV](https://beninwebtv.bj/en/benin-the-beninese-government-clarifies-the-reasons-for-the-extension-of-the-vodun-days-2027-in-ouidah/)).

At the time of writing (November 2026), the official site lists the headline elements but not a detailed day-by-day programme. Check it again in December before you fix your plans for each day.

## What happens

The official site lists four strands for 2027: a great Vodun ceremony attended by senior dignitaries, a music stage with a large concert and traditional music, street performances and the emergence of devotees from Ouidah's Vodun convents, and a Vodun Days village with stalls and entertainment ([vodundays.bj](https://www.vodundays.bj)).

The government's report on the 2026 edition gives a clearer picture of how that plays out across the town ([gouv.bj](https://www.gouv.bj/article/3406/succes-eclatant-vodun-days-2026-le-benin-assume-son-statut-de-grande-nation-de-culture-et-de-traditions-ancestrales/)):

- **By the sea.** The festival village opened near the Door of No Return, and a large open-air concert was held on the beach.
- **Across the town.** Rites took place in the Kpassè sacred forest and at the Mami-Dan temple. Zangbeto masks paraded on the esplanade of the French fort, and Egungun masquerades appeared on Place Maro.
- **Along the Route des Esclaves.** Sakpata rites were held along the old slave route, with processions recalling the slave trade.
- **The divination.** An ecumenical ceremony marked the annual Tofâ consultation, at which the divination sign for the country's coming year is revealed.
- **The finale.** Zangbeto and Egungun performances closed the festival at the Ouidah arena.

The two masquerades you are most likely to meet need a word of explanation. Egungun are understood as ancestor spirits who return to advise the living; their cloaks are layered in rich fabrics and can be extraordinarily ornate. Zangbeto are guardian spirits of the night, associated with protecting communities and keeping moral order; they appear as whirling cones of raffia, and who is inside is known only to initiates ([Afar](https://www.afar.com/magazine/what-is-vodun-how-benin-celebrates-its-spirit-religion)).

![Egungun masquerade costumes covered in sequinned panels, moving through a crowd in Benin](photo:egungun)

Not everything you see is staged for an audience. Some ceremonies are public; others happen inside convents and family compounds where outsiders are not expected. Concerts and the village are open to all. The line between the two is not always marked, which is why behaviour matters.

## How to behave and photograph respectfully

You are a guest at someone else's religious celebration. A few habits will make you a welcome one.

- **Ask before you photograph people, shrines and ceremonies.** A nod or a gesture towards your camera is usually enough to ask; a shake of the head is your answer. Some shrines and rites are not to be photographed at all.
- **Be especially careful with Zangbeto.** Their identities are meant to stay secret. Don't try to look or film inside the raffia, and don't follow a mask for a close-up.
- **Give processions space.** Don't walk through a ritual, step into the circle, or stand in front of devotees to get a better angle. Never touch masks or costumes.
- **Follow the lead of a guide or elder.** If you are with someone from the community, ask them what is appropriate before you raise a camera, and accept "no" without argument.
- **Dress modestly**, and expect to be asked to remove shoes or keep out of certain spaces.
- **Keep drones in the bag** unless you have explicit permission from the organisers and the community involved.

We'd also suggest leaving the camera down for a while at the start of each ceremony. You will understand more, and you will be better placed to ask.

## Booking accommodation early

This is the single most important practical step. The UK Foreign, Commonwealth & Development Office describes the festival as an annual public holiday in early January celebrated by most people in Benin, and advises making sure you have [arranged travel and accommodation](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/safety-and-security), because options are limited during the festival.

Ouidah itself is small, and rooms there go first. Many visitors stay in Cotonou or along the coast and travel in each day. With the 2027 edition stretching over a week, demand may spread more evenly, but we wouldn't count on it around the two public holidays. Book as soon as you have your dates, and confirm the booking directly with the property in December.

The official site also lists a Vodun Days camping option and a booking link ([vodundays.bj](https://www.vodundays.bj/infos-pratiques)), though at the time of writing it does not give details.

## Getting there from Cotonou

Ouidah is on the coast west of Cotonou, about thirty kilometres away by the government's reckoning ([gouv.bj](https://www.gouv.bj/article/1055/)); the festival site puts the drive at roughly an hour ([vodundays.bj](https://www.vodundays.bj)). International visitors fly into Cotonou's airport.

The official practical page mentions secure parking for those driving, ride-hailing services, and a public shuttle linking Cotonou and Ouidah for the event ([vodundays.bj](https://www.vodundays.bj/infos-pratiques)). Check closer to the time whether the shuttle runs for 2027 and from where.

Bear in mind the FCDO's wider transport advice. It describes driving standards and road conditions in Benin as poor, advises against driving outside towns at night because roads are poorly lit, and suggests avoiding taxis and long-distance buses, which it says are often poorly maintained and overloaded ([FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/safety-and-security)). In practice, a driver arranged through your hotel or a local operator is the simplest way to get back to Cotonou after a late concert.

## Crowds, heat and staying safe

The beach in front of the Door of No Return offers little shade, and you may be on your feet for hours. Bring a hat, sunscreen and more water than you think you need, and plan to rest in the middle of the day.

Crowds are part of the experience, and they bring the usual risks. The [FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/safety-and-security) warns specifically about pickpockets during the festival and more generally in places visited by international travellers and in crowded areas. Carry a copy of your passport rather than the original, keep your phone in a front pocket or zipped bag, and leave valuables at your hotel.

The beach needs respect too. The FCDO says ocean currents are very strong along all parts of Benin's coast and that many drownings occur each year. It advises not swimming unless you have reliable local advice and confidence in your ability, and not walking on the beach alone at any time ([FCDO](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/safety-and-security)). Advice changes, so check the [FCDO's Benin page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin), or your own government's, before you travel, along with your [entry requirements](/stories/benin-e-visa-entry-requirements/).

## Making more of your time in Ouidah

The festival fills the town, but the town is worth knowing on its own terms. Walk the [Route des Esclaves](/stories/ouidah-route-des-esclaves/) to the Door of No Return on a quieter morning, ideally with a local historian. Visit the Python Temple and the basilica facing it. If you have extra days, head east to [Ganvié](/stories/ganvie-village-on-stilts/) or north to the [royal palaces of Abomey](/stories/royal-palaces-of-abomey/), or fold the festival into a longer [10-day itinerary](/stories/benin-10-day-itinerary/).

## Planning your trip

Vodun Days rewards a little preparation: a room booked early, a driver you trust, and ideally a guide who can explain what you are seeing and tell you when to lower the camera. A local operator with ties to Ouidah's Vodun community can arrange all three.
