Late afternoon, and the heat is going out of the day. Across a field of stubble stands a house that looks like a small castle: round earth towers joined by curving walls, each tower capped with a pointed hat of thatch. A single low doorway faces you. Above it, on the flat roof between the towers, grain stores swell out like clay pots. Behind it all, the rocky ridges of the Atakora run away to the north-west.

This is a takienta, the family house of the Batammariba, better known to most visitors by the name "Tata Somba". It is one of the most striking buildings in West Africa. It is also somebody's home.

## Before anything else: the travel advice

The Tata Somba country lies in Benin's Atacora department, in the north-west. At the time of writing (November 2026), the UK FCDO [advises against all but essential travel](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/warnings-and-insurance) to the Atacora department (outside the zones where it advises against all travel), and against all travel to Pendjari National Park, its hunting zones and anywhere within 5km of the Burkina Faso border. Its [safety and security page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/safety-and-security) says the risk of kidnap is heightened in northern Benin. France Diplomatie also [warns against travel](https://www.diplomatie.gouv.fr/fr/conseils-aux-voyageurs/conseils-par-pays-destination/benin/) to Benin's northern border zones.

"All but essential" means a holiday does not count as essential. The FCDO also warns that your [insurance could be invalidated](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/benin/warnings-and-insurance) if you travel against its advice. Check your own government's advice, and check it again close to departure.

So read what follows as an introduction to a remarkable place, and as a guide for when the advice allows, not as an invitation to go now.

## The Batammariba and Koutammakou

The Batammariba, also referred to as Otammari, are the people who build these houses. Their homeland, which they call Koutammakou, straddles the border between north-west Benin and north-east Togo. According to the [UNESCO evaluation](https://whc.unesco.org/document/205650) of the Benin part, the first Batammariba settled in what is now Benin in the 6th century, and the Benin side holds the cradle of the people and major religious sites.

### A World Heritage site on both sides of the border

The UNESCO story has two chapters, and older guidebooks only tell the first.

- **2004, Togo.** The Togolese part, [Koutammakou, the Land of the Batammariba](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/1140/), was inscribed in July 2004 as a cultural landscape. It was Togo's first World Heritage site.
- **2023, Benin.** At its session in Saudi Arabia in September 2023, the World Heritage Committee approved a significant boundary modification ([decision 45 COM 8B.7](https://whc.unesco.org/en/decisions/8388)) that added Benin's part of Koutammakou to the Togolese property.

The [Government of Benin](https://www.gouv.bj/article/2358/) describes the result as a vast cross-border site of 271,826 hectares, 240,658 of them on the Benin side, known for its traditional architecture of Tata Somba. UNESCO's documents place the Benin part in the communes of Boukoumbé, Natitingou and Toucountouna, and say that with the extension, the whole of Koutammakou as the Batammariba recognise it is now protected.

## How a takienta works

![Close-up of a two-storey Tata Somba earth house with round towers and thatched turrets](photo:tata-somba-house)

A takienta (plural sikien) is a two-storey earth tower-house. UNESCO's description of the site sets out how it is organised:

- **The ground floor** is partly given over to animals.
- **An upper floor** sits above, reached in different ways depending on the type of house.
- **An outdoor terrace** sits between them, and this is where the granaries stand: near-spherical stores that swell out above cylindrical bases.
- **The roofs** are either flat or topped with steep conical thatch that rises above the walls.

The houses vary by type: in the shape of the roof, how you reach the upper floor, and how many rooms there are. UNESCO counts several thousand sikien across Koutammakou, with around 1,400 still lived in on the Benin side.

### More than a house

UNESCO stresses that a takienta is not only a dwelling. It is also a temple dedicated to the ancestors, and its layout has strong associations with the human body: the door is seen as the mouth, the windows as eyes, the granary as the stomach, and the decoration on the walls is linked to scarification on the skin.

That is the most important thing to understand before you visit. You are not looking at an open-air museum. You are standing at someone's front door, which is also their place of worship.

### Earth, wood and weather

Sikien are built of earth and need regular care. Heavy rains in 2018 brought [partial or complete collapse](https://whc.unesco.org/en/news/1932) of houses on the Togo side, with the loss of food and livestock; UNESCO's [2023 state of conservation report](https://whc.unesco.org/en/soc/4398) says the damaged houses have since been restored. Benin's own reporting to UNESCO mentions reforestation campaigns to address a shortage of the wood used in building, and notes that a joint Benin–Togo management body has yet to be set up.

We have not found an official source describing the building process step by step, so we will not invent one here. If you visit with a local guide, ask: who builds, when, and how a house is maintained are questions people are often glad to answer, in their own words.

## Visiting respectfully

When advice allows, the sensible way to visit Tata Somba country is through a local guide or an operator who knows the families. Some arrangements include staying with a household; Escale Voyages' northern itinerary, for example, includes a family-hosted night at Koussoukoingou. A few things matter more here than almost anywhere else in Benin:

- **Go with someone local.** A guide or host can introduce you, explain what you are seeing and tell you what is off limits.
- **Ask before you enter, and before you photograph.** That goes for people, houses and especially shrines. If the answer is no, it is no.
- **Do not touch shrines or offerings,** and do not climb onto roofs or terraces unless invited.
- **Agree any contribution openly** with your guide or host beforehand, rather than handing out cash or sweets to children.
- **Dress modestly** and be ready to sit, greet and take your time. Visits here are slow by design.

Benin's government registered tourism guides in October 2026, according to our sister site [Trusted Ground](https://trustedground.tourground.com/articles/benin-abomey-ouidah/); asking whether your guide is registered is reasonable.

## What about the Togo side?

Because Koutammakou crosses the border, the Togolese part is sometimes suggested as an alternative. Before you consider it, read the FCDO's Togo advice as carefully as its Benin advice.

At the time of writing, the FCDO [advises against all travel](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/togo) to within 30km of Togo's border with Burkina Faso (except the city of Dapaong and the N1 highway leading to it from the south), and against all but essential travel to the rest of the Savanes region. Koutammakou lies in the Kara region, further south, which the FCDO does not name in its area warnings. But its [safety and security page](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/togo/safety-and-security) says terrorists are very likely to try to carry out attacks in Togo, with the armed group JNIM mainly active in the north near the Burkina Faso border.

In other words, the Togo side is not covered by the same "all but essential" warning, but it is still in the north of a country with a live security risk. If you consider it:

- Approach from Lomé in the south rather than crossing from Benin's Atacora.
- Check the FCDO's current map, since the two Togo pages have not always used the same distance from the border.
- Get your paperwork in order: the FCDO says British nationals need a [Togo e-visa](https://www.gov.uk/foreign-travel-advice/togo/entry-requirements) applied for in advance, with no visa on arrival, and a yellow fever certificate.

## If the north is off the table

Benin's south carries its own deep history, and you can build a full trip around it while the advice stands. The [royal palaces of Abomey](/stories/royal-palaces-of-abomey/) tell the story of the Dahomey kingdom; [Ouidah's Route des Esclaves](/stories/ouidah-route-des-esclaves/) follows the path of the Atlantic slave trade to the sea; and [Ganvié](/stories/ganvie-village-on-stilts/) shows another way of building a home around a landscape, this time on stilts over a lake. Our [10-day itinerary](/stories/benin-10-day-itinerary/) links them together.

When the north does reopen to travellers on official advice, the Tata Somba country will reward the long drive. Talk to an operator in Cotonou who works with the families in the Atakora, and ask them how they read the advice before you book.
